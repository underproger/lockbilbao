199	A snapped key is a common problem, and it’s most often made worse by trying to get the piece out yourself. Here’s what to do straight away and what’s best avoided.
200	<h3>Why do keys snap in the lock?</h3>
201	<p>Most often through wear: over time the key thins and cracks near the bow. Another cause is trying to force the key when the lock is sticking. Thin mailbox and padlock keys snap especially often.</p>
202	<p>If the key starts to turn stiffly, don’t wait for it to snap: clean and lubricate the lock or book a <a href="{url:svc-reparacion}">lock repair</a>.</p>
203	<h3>What should you avoid if the key has snapped?</h3>
204	<p>Don’t put the spare key into the lock — it will push the piece in further. Don’t try to get the piece out with glue: it will set inside the mechanism and the cylinder will have to be replaced. Hairpins, needles and tweezers damage the pins more often than they help.</p>
205	<p>If the door is open, don’t close it until the piece has been removed.</p>
206	<h3>Can the cylinder be saved after key extraction?</h3>
207	<p>Yes, if the mechanism isn’t damaged. A piece that snapped through wear and wasn’t pushed in usually comes out without damage. If the cylinder is damaged, the locksmith will suggest replacing it in the same visit. Find out more in the section <a href="#no-damage">on extraction without replacing the cylinder</a>.</p>
208	<h3>Can a new key be cut from the broken pieces?</h3>
209	<p>For standard keys, often yes: a key-cutting shop can make a copy from the two parts. High-security keys can only be copied with the owner’s card. If you have no keys left, it’s safer to <a href="{url:svc-cambio}">replace the cylinder</a>.</p>
